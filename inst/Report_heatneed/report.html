Heating needs

Author

Márton Kiss, MD

Introduction

  

In this document I’m describing a possible method for estimating the expected amount of energy required for heating left for a given year (or season as my billing cycle starts annually on August 1st, during the local summer).

I was motivated by my local billing method which has a huge discount for natural gas but only up to a given amount. I can supplement my heating with air conditioning, but that is more expensive than the discounted gas (but not more expensive than the full-priced gas). The efficiency of the air conditioning is also impacted by the outside temperature, COP (amount of heating / amount of electricity used) being higher when the outside temperature is higher.

Bottom line is, I’d like to use a given amount of natural gas for heating per year, and to top off the remaining heat requirements with air conditioning, preferably during autumn & spring.

For better or worse I feel that a considerable amount of my professional pride rests in tracking & determining the optimal method of heating.

Description of the data

 
Meteorological data from 1995 onward is available for free at meteostat for my location in Budapest, Hungary (Europe) in excel. I haven’t figured out the API, and I am downloading a new excel periodically and concatenating them in R.

I have also read my gas meter regularly from 2018 onwards. To make it interesting, I don’t read the meter regularly, so the times between readings are between 2.4 hours and 188 days so the quality of the observations are not ideal.

Quantifying cold and long winters

The amount of energy required for heating is a function of the outside temperature.

Therefore to predict the energy needs left in a given year, we should make a prediction about the expected amount of energy required until December 31.

Assume a constant indoor temperature of 20 °C. Heat loss is linear to the inside-outside temperature difference. Therefore when the outside temperature is 16 °C we need 4x the power than if it were 19 °C. For a given year we add up the “missing degrees” below 20 for every day — the cumulative heating need in °C·days.

We are neglecting solar radiation and building heat capacity. For simplicity, assume that at each day the distribution of cumulative heating needs across years is approximately normal.

A GLS model with a natural spline (8 df) captures the seasonal curve. An exponential variance structure accounts for increasing spread as the year progresses.

Figuring in actual energy usage

We track the ratio of gas consumed to cumulative heating need (m³ per °C·day). If this ratio stays stable, we can predict total annual gas usage by combining it with the conditional total heat need forecast.

Efficiency tracking (Jan–Dec)

Rather than a flat daily budget, we track the gas consumption efficiency — how many m³ of gas per °C·day of heating need. This ratio should stay roughly stable. If it climbs, we are over-heating; if it drops, the heat pump is picking up slack.

The dashed grey lines show the predicted efficiency band based on conditional forecasts of total heat need.

The solid colored line shows actual gas usage efficiency, colored by remaining gas budget. When the line stays within the grey dashed prediction bands, the budget is on track.
